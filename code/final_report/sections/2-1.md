In the {{num:n_basins}} basins studied ({{num:basin_area_range_km2}}) a river can rise above its high-flow level the day after heavy rain, so a warning is useful only if it is issued a day or more ahead. The test years {{num:split_test_years_text}} hold {{num:events_test_years}} high-flow events; {{num:events_one_day}} lasted one day and the median event {{num:events_median_days}} days, so a second-day warning often comes too late. Of the {{num:high_flow_days_test}} high-flow days, {{num:high_flow_days_second_or_later}} ({{num:high_flow_days_second_or_later_share}}) are the second or a later day of an event, which "tomorrow = today" warns of without any model; the warning that matters is the onset (defined in Section 2.3). The events are not only hurricanes: {{num:events_tropical}} followed a tropical cyclone; the rest came from warm-season storms and cool-season weather.

Long short-term memory (LSTM) networks simulate daily flow well [@kratzert2018], and one LSTM trained on hundreds of catchments beats calibrated hydrological models [@kratzert2019], carries skill to catchments it never saw [@kratzert2019pub] and runs as a global operational flood-forecasting system [@nearing2024]. Those scores are simulation skill or return-period reliability on large gauge sets, not onsets warned and false warnings on a small humid basin by a forecast that reads only what exists at the issue time. Their research rain table, the CAMELS daily Daymet precipitation (here called the rain table), closes its day in the morning and files later rain under the next day, an offset the data papers note [@thornton2021], [@kratzert2021] but never price for a warning. That gap, model or information, is measured here with controls and judged by criteria fixed in advance.
