The model class is the LSTM [@hochreiter1997], a recurrent network whose gates carry the state of a wet or dry catchment over months. Kratzert et al. first applied it to CAMELS [@newman2015], [@addor2017]: on {{num:pub_kratzert2018_n_basins}} catchments an LSTM per basin reached a mean NSE of {{num:pub_kratzert2018_mean_nse_lstm}} against {{num:pub_kratzert2018_mean_nse_sac_sma}} for the calibrated SAC-SMA model [@kratzert2018]. The regional design copied here, one network for {{num:pub_kratzert2019_n_basins}} basins with static attributes and a basin-averaged NSE loss, reached a median NSE of {{num:pub_kratzert2019_lstm_with_basin_properties_nse_loss_single}} ({{num:pub_kratzert2019_lstm_with_basin_properties_nse_loss_ensemble}} as an ensemble) [@kratzert2019], carries skill to basins that supplied no training data [@kratzert2019pub] and, a position paper argues, should never be trained on a single basin [@kratzert2024]: hence one shared network, static properties and several seeds (Section 5.2). Feng et al. added the latest gauge reading and raised the median NSE on {{num:pub_feng2020_n_basins}} basins from {{num:pub_feng2020_projection_no_observed_flow_671}} to {{num:pub_feng2020_with_flow_observed_1_day_before_671}} [@feng2020]; Gauch et al. fed hourly inputs to one multi-timescale LSTM [@gauch2021]. Studies [@kratzert2018], [@kratzert2019], [@kratzert2019pub], [@feng2020] and [@gauch2021] report NSE in simulation with the weather of the day estimated, so they belong beside the perfect-weather rows here ({{num:fw_upper}}). Forecast studies score otherwise: a global LSTM driven by weather forecasts reports return-period reliability [@nearing2024], an LSTM fed forecast variables beat the physics-based GloFAS forecasts at western US gauges [@hunt2022], GloFAS itself warns when ensemble forecasts exceed climatological thresholds [@alfieri2013], and [@frame2022] reports accuracy on withheld extremes. None reports onsets warned and false warnings at a fixed issue time, the measure used here.

The day stamp of rain appears in the data literature as a quality matter: Daymet V4 moved the rain of stations observing before noon back one day [@thornton2021], and the CAMELS forcing predates V4; a one-day lag between Daymet and the Maurer forcing [@maurer2002] is reported in [@kratzert2021], and gauge reporting times matter against Stage IV [@beck2019]. The added products are NLDAS-2 [@xia2012], whose CAMELS daily values end in the afternoon (measured here, Section 4.2); ERA5 [@hersbach2020]; AORC [@fall2023]; Stage IV, the real-time radar-gauge analysis [@nelson2016]; and the GEFSv12 reforecast [@guan2022]. The scores are NSE [@nash1970] and the Kling-Gupta efficiency (KGE) [@gupta2009]. The gap (Table T4) is narrow and measurable: none of these studies prices the day end of the research rain table ({{num:day_end_daymet}} local time, measured here) for a one-day warning on small basins, tests rain to the issue time against a control over the table's own window, or judges the result by a warning rule fixed in advance.
